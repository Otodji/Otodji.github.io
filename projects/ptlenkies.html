The problem

Why a new distribution?

Classical models such as the Weibull or Burr XII distributions often struggle to capture the shapes found in real engineering and biological data: strong skewness, sharp peaks or heavy tails. The PTLEN family, introduced in Open Physics (2023), adds shape parameters on top of a baseline distribution to gain this flexibility while keeping a closed-form distribution function.

PTLENKies turns this research into a ready-to-use R package, so any statistician can evaluate, simulate and fit the distribution in a few lines of code, without rewriting the maths.

The model

Distribution function

For \(x > 0\) and \(m, n, p, \lambda, \beta, c, k > 0\):

$$F(x) = \exp\!\Big( m\,n \big(1 - K(x)^{-p}\big) \Big)\Big[\, 2 - \exp\!\Big( n \big(1 - K(x)^{-p}\big) \Big) \Big]^{m}$$ $$K(x) = 1 - \exp\!\left( -\lambda \left( \frac{1 - (1+x^{c})^{-k}}{(1+x^{c})^{-k}} \right)^{\beta} \right)$$

The parameters \(c\) and \(k\) come from the Burr XII baseline; \(m, n, p, \lambda, \beta\) control the shape.

Try it

Interactive density explorer

Move the sliders to see how each parameter reshapes the distribution. The curves are computed live in your browser from the formula above. Press Simulate to draw a random sample by inverse-transform sampling.

The package ships the GlassFibers dataset: breaking strengths of 1.5 cm glass fibres measured at the UK National Physical Laboratory.

Quick start

Three lines to fit real data

install.packages("PTLENKies")
library(PTLENKies)

# Density and cumulative probabilities
x <- c(0.5, 1.0, 1.5)
P_PTLENKBurr(x, m = 1, n = 1, p = 1, lambda = 0.5, beta = 1.5, c = 2, k = 1)

# Fit the seven parameters to the glass-fibre data and plot the fit
data(GlassFibers)
fit <- E_PTLENKBurr(GlassFibers)
Sim_PTLENKBurr(GlassFibers)
